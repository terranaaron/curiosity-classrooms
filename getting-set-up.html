Phase 5

Community — later, truly

Nothing to do now. Read when curious.

What Slack actually is, in one breath: a private group-chat space for a set of invited people, organized into topic "channels" (one for lesson ideas, one for adoption questions, one for announcements). Think of it as a faculty lounge that lives on your computer and phone. You would create a space, name it Curiosity Classrooms, put your logo on it, and invite people — free to start.

The calm rule: you don't need a community platform until roughly twenty people keep asking to talk to each other — not just to you. Until that day, your "community" is simpler and better: an email list people can join, plus a monthly hour on Zoom.

The ladder (climb one rung at a time, change your mind freely):

  • Rung 1 — an email list. A monthly note from you: one good idea, one resource, one date. This is the whole community engine for year one.
  • Rung 2 — a monthly roundtable. One Zoom hour, one topic, whoever comes.
  • Rung 3 — a Slack space, when people want to keep talking between roundtables. Free tier is plenty to start (note: it only shows the last ~90 days of messages).
  • Rung 4 — a paid community platform (Circle, Mighty Networks, Heartbeat — roughly $50–90/month) if it thrives and you want a space that feels fully "yours," with your branding throughout. Moving a small, healthy community up a rung is a weekend, not a crisis.

Phase 6

Word of mouth, by design

Read only — this is a conversation for you and Aaron, not a task list.

Your three audiences already talk to each other — teachers to their science leads, leads to their peers in other divisions, superintendents to other superintendents. The plan is to make each of those conversations one click easier:

  • Every reader gets a "forward this" tool. The teacher page already drafts an email to their science lead. The same trick works sideways: a lead forwarding to a peer lead, a superintendent to a neighboring division.
  • The roundtable "+1 rule." Every attendee may bring one colleague. The gentlest referral engine there is — no one is selling anything, and the room grows itself.
  • Rewards flow to schools, never to individuals. Public-school employees generally can't accept personal thank-you gifts — so a referral thank-you looks like "a free workshop hour for your teachers," never a gift card. Gracious, and keeps everyone comfortable.
  • Ask for the sentence, not the favor. After a good engagement, request one quotable sentence and permission to name the division. Testimonials do the referring while you sleep.
  • Say yes to the conference stage. One session at a state science-educators conference reaches more leads than a year of cold emails.
